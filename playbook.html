// PRE-GAME · WHY THIS BRIDGE EXISTS
If you can read a box score, you can read a security posture.

Cybersecurity has a reputation problem. It sounds like a wall of acronyms thrown up by people who don't want you in the room — SIEM, EDR, MFA, NIST, CMMC. So most people tune out, decide it's "the IT team's problem," and click the link in the email anyway. That tuning-out is exactly how breaches happen. Security isn't a back-office chore; it's a team sport, and every person on the roster either makes a play or gives one up.

Sports already taught the whole world how to think about this. A team puts points on the board (offense) and stops the other team from scoring (defense). It studies its opponent before the game (scouting), reviews what went wrong after (game film), runs set plays from a playbook, and plays inside a rulebook enforced by refs. Swap a few nouns and you've described a modern security operation almost perfectly.

Attackers are the away team. Your defenders are the home team. The data is the trophy. Everything else is just play-calling.

That's what this page is — a translator. Below, you'll find an interactive glossary that maps sports lingo to its cybersecurity counterpart, a side-by-side playbook of offense (how attackers score) versus defense (how teams stop them), and a quick quiz to see if the lingo stuck. No prior knowledge required. If you've ever yelled at a TV over a bad call, you already have the instincts.

// THE PLAYBOOK · OFFENSE VS. DEFENSE
How points get scored — and stopped.

In security, the offense is the attacker (and the "red team" who simulates them). The defense is your "blue team." Here are the signature plays on each side of the ball.

▲ OFFENSE — How attackers score

The Trick Play (Phishing)

A fake handoff. The attacker disguises a malicious email or text as something you trust — your coach, your bank, the league office — to make you hand over the ball (your password).

The Blitz (Brute Force / DDoS)

Send everything at once. Overwhelm the line by flooding a login or a website with so much traffic it collapses or cracks.

Stealing Signs (Reconnaissance)

Before the snap, study the defense. Attackers map your network, scan for open ports, and read your "signals" to find the weak spot.

The Hail Mary (Zero-Day)

An unscouted play nobody has a defense for yet — an exploit for a flaw the defenders don't even know exists.

The Inside Job (Insider Threat)

A player on your own roster throwing the game — a disgruntled or careless insider who already has a jersey and locker access.

▼ DEFENSE — How teams stop it

Zone Defense (Segmentation)

Don't leave the whole field open. Split the network into zones so a breach in one area can't run the length of the field untouched.

Two Defenders on the Ball (MFA)

One lock isn't enough. Multi-factor authentication requires a second proof — a code, a tap, a fingerprint — so a stolen password alone can't score.

Watching Game Film (Logging & Monitoring)

Every snap is recorded. Security logs let defenders replay exactly what happened, spot the missed assignment, and adjust before the rematch.

The Goalkeeper (Endpoint Detection)

Last line at every device. EDR sits on laptops and phones, watching for the shot that beat everyone else and stopping it at the net.

The Two-Minute Drill (Incident Response)

A rehearsed plan for when you're losing. When a breach hits, the team runs a practiced drill — contain, eradicate, recover — instead of improvising.
